01 / THE ATTRACTION EDIT

Are Our Dating Preferences Really Our Own?

The Social Psychology of Attraction in Online Dating

The question: How do socialization, social norms, impression management, and social hierarchies influence what people consider attractive in potential romantic partners?

You open a dating app and find two profiles. The first person lists their career accomplishments, talking about their goals for the future. The second person speaks more about their personality, hobbies, sense of humor, and the kind of relationship they have with their friends.

Within a few seconds of reading each of these profiles, you may have already decided which of these people you would like to get to know better.

But why exactly is that, and what does it say about our perception of others?

Most people consider their preferences regarding a potential partner to be completely personal. Some prefer older partners, while others prefer younger partners. Many even have a specific "type". But where does this come from? How do our preferences develop?

As we get older, we slowly start to build a picture of what we want our partner to be like. This includes qualities our partners should have, which are communicated by our families, our friends, social media, and our culture. We take all of these suggestions and incorporate them into our idealized image, which influences what we look for later on.

As we internalize messages that define desirable characteristics, they can begin to feel like our own preferences. We may no longer even realize that there are alternatives.

With online dating, individuals can look for potential partners in a large pool of possible candidates. For many users of online dating services, however, decisions about whom to initiate contact with are made on the basis of a few paragraphs prepared by the individuals themselves that highlight certain aspects of their personalities.

By taking a closer look at the way society portrays potential partners and expectations in relationships, we can explore this question more closely. Does society influence the choices we make when dating, or are our preferences completely our own?

02 / THE SOCIAL PSYCHOLOGY OF ATTRACTION

Who taught you your type?

Four concepts to help frame the question.

01

Socialization

Socialization is the process by which individuals acquire beliefs, values, practices, and expectations that are shared by members of a given society.

The way one perceives and evaluates attraction to another can be shaped by ideas originating from childhood. These are frequently influenced by parents, friends, media such as television and films, the internet, as well as various social networks.

An example of this socialization is someone who was told growing up that it was really important to have a partner who had financial stability. Another example is someone who heard growing up that a person who was physically attractive or had high social status was desirable.

Later in life, we may incorporate these expectations into our own standards for the romantic partners we choose, often without even realizing we are doing so.

This, however, does not mean that socialization entirely determines our preferences for others. Our personal traits, experiences, and relationships can all have an impact on our preferences.

By learning why we link certain characteristics with attractiveness, compatibility, and successful relationships, socialization helps us understand our dating preferences better.

Even preferences that we consider completely our own can be socially influenced.

02

Social norms & gender roles

First, there is the concept of social norms and how these influence people's behavior and how they expect other people to behave. We then explore the closely related topic of gender roles.

Traditionally, men have been perceived as financial providers, while women have been perceived as nurturing and caring for others.

These expectations can often influence what people look for in a potential partner. For example, financial success, drive, or ambition may be associated with traditionally masculine qualities. On the other hand, emotional warmth, kindness, and nurturing may be associated with traditionally feminine qualities.

Expectations for both men's and women's preferences in romantic partners can vary within cultures, change over time, and differ across social environments and countries.

Research across different countries has found that differences in men's and women's reported preferences for romantic partners tend to be smaller in societies with greater gender equality (Zentner and Mitura 2012).

Again, it is important to note that this does not necessarily mean that men and women want completely different things in a potential partner.

Rather than concluding that men and women seek completely different characteristics in a partner, it is more reasonable to suggest that social expectations regarding gender can influence what people believe they are looking for in a partner.

TRY IT / FIRST IMPRESSIONS

Different profiles. First impressions.

Most people reveal different aspects of themselves depending on how they want others to see them.

Online dating allows individuals to highlight the aspects of their personality that they think will present them more favorably to potential romantic partners.

Imagine coming across two fictional dating profiles.

One person's dating profile emphasizes their professional life, financial status, and goals.

The second profile focuses on the person's personality, sense of humor, relationships with friends, family, and how they spend their free time.

Which person would you be more interested in getting to know?

These profiles are fictional.

PROFILE A

A plan worth building.

I’m an architect with a master’s degree and a steady role at a design studio. Right now, I’m working on a community library. Turning a complicated brief into something useful is my kind of challenge. I take my work seriously, but I’m looking for a long-term relationship too. Building a future with someone matters to me as much as building a career.

PROFILE B

Dinner comes with terrible puns.

Once a month, my friends take over my dinner table. I cook, they provide commentary, and nobody escapes the bad puns. I’m usually the person a friend calls when they need someone to listen. At the moment, I’m training for a charity 10K, so a slow run counts as a successful morning. I’d love a long-term relationship with good conversation and room for ordinary days together.

Which profile appeals to you more?

Choose Profile A or Profile B before revealing.

Both profiles describe the same person.

Meet Alex, a fictional 29-year-old architect. Alex has a master’s degree, a steady studio job, and a community-library project. Alex also hosts monthly dinners, tells terrible puns, listens to friends, and is training for a charity 10K. Alex wants a long-term relationship. Every detail in both profiles belongs to this one person.

Here is the interesting part.

Both profiles describe the exact same person.

The first profile highlights this individual's career accomplishments, ambition, and financial goals for the future.

The second focuses on humor, personality, friendships, and personal interests.

Both profiles can be completely true. However, they may give different first impressions of the same person.

This demonstrates the concept of impression management.

03

Impression management

Impression management is the way people manage how others form impressions of them.

We are managing our impressions on a regular basis. For example, we are likely to behave differently at a job interview than we would if we were around our closest friends.

Online dating is another area in which individuals can control how they are perceived by potential partners.

For example, anybody on a dating site could select photos, write descriptions, and accentuate certain aspects of their personality or accomplishments.

Imagine someone creating two different dating profiles.

In the first example, the individual focuses on highlighting their career, education, income, and goals for the future.

They then concentrate on describing their kind and gentle nature, funny side, friendships with others, and personal interests in the second profile.

Both of these profiles describe the same person, but they give very different first impressions.

This does not mean that the individual is being dishonest. They are presenting an accurate version of themselves, but they are highlighting different aspects that they think will present them in a positive light.

Research has found that people work hard to present the most desirable version of themselves to others while trying to remain true to who they really are (Ellison, Heino, and Gibbs 2006).

Is it the whole person that we are attracted to, or just the little things that they are sharing with us?

04

Social identity & stratification

Social identity refers to the different ways in which people perceive themselves and others based on their membership in different social groups.

Social stratification refers to resources, opportunities, and status in society that are distributed unequally.

Both concepts can help explain why some individuals seem to receive more romantic attention than others.

For instance, race, education, and income can all be factors that people take into account when they are searching for potential partners.

A person with a higher level of education may search for other people with a similar level of education, as they may have more shared experiences and interests.

This way of dating can reinforce existing social inequalities between social classes.

Race provides another example.

Research on online dating has identified different patterns of racial exclusion among White men and women and how they express preferences for potential partners online (Feliciano, Robnett, and Komaie 2009).

Thus, patterns of racial exclusion and preferences on online dating sites may be influenced by racial stereotypes as well as existing social hierarchies.

However, that does not mean that all preferences for certain characteristics are discriminatory.

The points of interest for us are the ways in which social environments and structures can affect our preferences.

03 / RESEARCH, IMPLICATIONS & REFERENCES

What does the research say?

Scholarly evidence

Studies of human romantic attraction, including those that investigate online dating, have found that individuals' preferences for potential partners are associated with a variety of factors, including their individual experiences, how they perceive others, the broader social environment, and how they present themselves.

Previous research has examined the ways in which people manage their dating profiles to create an impression of themselves that they believe will attract potential partners. In one study of 34 online daters, participants described attempting to present themselves in the most positive light while also conveying a sense of sincerity to potential dates (Ellison, Heino, and Gibbs 2006).

Another study found that the characteristics individuals reported wanting in a romantic partner did not reliably predict attraction in a speed dating scenario (Eastwick and Finkel 2008).

Although participants reported certain preferences for potential romantic partners, there was little agreement between these preferences and the partners toward whom participants experienced initial attraction.

In countries with greater gender equality, reported gender differences in romantic preferences tended to be smaller (Zentner and Mitura 2012).

This is also important when considering the social conditions that influence both men and women in their choice of partners.

Studies of online dating have also found patterns of racial exclusion (Feliciano, Robnett, and Komaie 2009). Racial stereotypes and social identities may influence how people evaluate potential partners and whether they choose to initiate contact with someone. Inequality can also exist in online dating, just as it does in other aspects of life.

Studies about partner choice have also found that characteristics such as education and other personal attributes can influence romantic preferences and choices (Hitsch, Hortaçsu, and Ariely 2010).

A study of heterosexual online dating markets in four American cities examined hierarchies of desirability that shape dating interactions (Bruch and Newman 2018).

Some individuals receive considerably more romantic attention than others, which means there is no reason to assume that romantic opportunities are distributed evenly.

Psychological research on online dating also raises questions about the extent to which online dating systems can accurately identify and bring together compatible partners (Finkel et al. 2012).

From the studies above, it is clear that having a personal type can’t simply be explained.

These studies use different methods and populations. Therefore, they do not establish that society determines each individual's preferred partners.

However, studies like these highlight the complex relationship between individual preferences and social influences, which can be difficult to separate.

Implications & takeaways

Understanding the social psychology of attraction helps us make sense of our preferences and perhaps even question some of them.

You may find that you have a type but then wonder where that preference comes from. Have you developed it through past experiences? Does your family expect you to choose someone with certain characteristics, or does society place certain expectations on you when it comes to finding a romantic partner?

Recognizing these influences does not mean you have to be with someone you are not attracted to.

Attraction, compatibility, personal boundaries, and individual preferences are all important pieces of relationships, and they should be recognized and respected.

Understanding where our preferences come from helps us distinguish between what is important to us and what we have accepted as normal because of society.

This can also be related to the role of dating applications in people's search for romantic partners and relationships.

Dating platforms provide users with images and other information about potential partners, including their education, jobs, interests, and more.

This can provide helpful information about another person, but it can also encourage people to make judgments very quickly, which might not always be fair.

For this reason, how dating apps are developed could influence how people form romantic connections and may reinforce existing social hierarchies.

Do dating apps create romantic opportunities, or do they also serve to reinforce social hierarchies?

Understanding the influence of stereotypes also means recognizing them more easily and questioning their influence when making judgments about others.

Studying the social psychology of attraction is not about dictating whom you can or should be interested in or fall in love with.

The study of human attraction can help us understand why we find certain characteristics more desirable than others and how much our preferences may be influenced by individual experiences, stereotypes, and other social influences.

Perhaps the more interesting question is not simply, "What is my type?"

It is also, "Who or what taught me to have that type in the first place?"
